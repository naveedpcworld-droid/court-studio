# Competitor Commentary Pattern Report

**Scope:** 14 separate commentary channels, collected in Ego Light. This folder is not merged with the official-court library.

**Dataset:** 3,017 long-form videos · 2,998 transcripts · 14 channels · 17.3M transcript words.

## Short answer

1. **The winning genre is cop / civil-rights courtroom conflict, not generic sentencing.** 43 of the top 100 videos are cop-misconduct stories, versus only 148 of 3,017 titles overall. Cop/police titles have **11.8× the median views** and **14.7× the median views-per-day** of the channel baseline.
2. **Judge-brand channels are volume machines built on a few livestream courts.** Judge Boyd appears in 544 titles, Raquel West in 457, Judge Stevens in 100, Fleischer in 81, Simpson in 70 and Oakley in 68. The channels do not find random cases — they monitor repeat judges and mine the same dockets.
3. **Generic sentencing is overused and underperforms.** It is 28.9% of titles but only 0.75× baseline views; it is just 9% of the top 100. The exception is a specific number or quote attached to a human story.
4. **Sovereign-citizen content is a reliable volume genre but not the highest-lift genre.** It is 16.4% of titles, median 0.91× baseline. It works when there is an absurd quote or a visible judge reaction, not just the label.
5. **Titles are specific, conversational and front-loaded.** Top titles use all-caps emphasis (70/100), exclamation (51/100), a named authority or cop (42/100), and a concrete trigger. Vague “sentenced in court” titles are the losing pattern.
6. **Thumbnails are quote cards plus faces.** 56 of 60 top thumbnails contained overlay text. The dominant grammar: 3–6 word white/yellow uppercase quote, a red arrow pointing at the speaker, two-panel split (judge + defendant/cop/bodycam), and a close-up reaction face.

### What the top 100 have in common

- **43/100** have a cop/civil-rights theme in the title; only **4.9%** of all titles do.
- **9/100** have a generic sentencing theme; **28.9%** of all titles do — the mass market is saturated and the top is not.
- **42/100** have “cop / police” as a title feature; **45/100** name a judge, versus 88% of all titles. The biggest hits often use the cop/attorney as the protagonist, not the judge.
- **70/100** use all-caps emphasis; **51/100** use an exclamation; **30/100** use two or more all-caps words.
- Titles are usually a **two-part story**: specific trigger, then consequence. Examples: *“Judge Orders Attorney's Arrest but it IMMEDIATELY Backfires!”*, *“Sovereign Citizen Keeps Saying ‘Overstand’ — Judge Doesn’t Put Up with Nonsense”*, *“Defendant Keeps Insulting Witnesses in Court Hallway — Judge Revokes Bond Immediately.”*

**Winning patterns:**

1. `[Cop/Attorney/Defendant] [specific action] — [consequence]!` → *Cop Gets EXPOSED by His Own Bodycam — Testimony BACKFIRES in Court!*
2. `Sovereign Citizen [absurd claim] — Judge [reaction]!` → *Sovereign Citizen Keeps Saying “Overstand” — Judge Doesn’t Put Up with Nonsense*
3. `Judge [Name] [reaction verb] [subject] After [trigger]` → *Judge Boyd Watched the Video and Ruled*
4. `[Identity] [bad decision] — Judge [sentence]!` → *Obstinate Mom Defies Every Order — Gets Brutal Sentence She Didn't Expect!!*
5. `When [authority] [does X] ...` → *When the Judge Saves His People from CORRUPT COPS!*

**Avoid:** generic “sentenced”, “court hearing”, “judge rules”, no quote/trigger, no specific number, and titles that describe the whole 2-hour stream instead of one 20-second exchange.

## 4. Thumbnail pattern

60 top thumbnails by views were downloaded; **56/60** had OCR-readable overlay text. The most frequent words were: **this** (15), **the** (10), **you** (9), **what** (9), **looks** (8), **like** (8), **that** (6), **cops** (6), **case** (6), **law** (5), **cop** (5), **was** (5), **judge** (5), **not** (4).

**Visual grammar:**

- 3–6 words, white or yellow, heavy sans-serif, all caps, often a **first-person quote** (“Arrest him right now!”, “I’m sick of bad cops!”, “That is not the law!”).
- **Red arrows** pointing at the person being discussed; often 2–3 arrows in one image.
- **Two-panel split**: judge/attorney reaction on one side, defendant/cop bodycam on the other.
- **Close-up faces** with exaggerated expressions; high contrast; courtroom background kept visible for authenticity.
- Text often repeats the spoken quote, not the video title. The title supplies context; the thumbnail supplies the emotional quote.

### How to turn one hearing into a competitor-style video

1. Pick a **single exchange**, not the whole stream. The clip should have a clean power reversal: authority figure vs defendant/cop/attorney.
2. Verify the **case name, charge, judge, and disposition** from the transcript + video. Do not use an ASR name without checking.
3. Extract **one quotable line** (6–12 words) and one **specific outcome** (years, bond, dismissal, contempt, acquittal).
4. Title in 2 parts: `specific trigger — consequence/reaction!` Use cop/sovereign/contempt only if that is genuinely the story.
5. Thumbnail: one face + one prop/bodycam panel; 3–5 word quote; red arrow; yellow/white bold text. Do not put the whole title on the image.
6. Test the first 15 seconds: quote → context → stakes → reaction. Competitor winners do this without a long intro.
